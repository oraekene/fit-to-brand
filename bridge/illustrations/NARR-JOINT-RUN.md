# Narrative 1 — How SunJar found its buyers before it found its look

*Companion to `ILLU-JOINT-RUN.md` (granular walkthrough) and `data-joint/` (miniature
artifacts). Same phases, same artifacts, told as the story of the team that ran them.
All names, places, and figures are fictional and illustrative.*

Adaeze had a warehouse of finished hubs and a problem she could feel but not yet name.
SunJar's SHS-200 worked — five pilot homes had run four lamps and two phones for a
fortnight without touching kerosene — but every conversation about the launch dissolved
into taste. The designer wanted matte charcoal because it looked premium. The sales
lead wanted blue because the rival LuminaHome kits on dealer shelves were blue and
blue clearly sold. The agent coordinator wanted whatever photographed best on a phone,
because agents sold over WhatsApp. Three opinions, no referee.

The joint run gave them one. Its first ruling was the least glamorous and the most
consequential: before anyone was allowed an opinion about the look, the team had to
write down what the thing was. The S0 freeze took a week of arguing and produced two
documents — a short normative spec and a fat appendix of source dumps — and a list
that would govern the next six months: Out-of-Scope. Grid-tied installs, refrigeration,
mains-wired loads, school fixed wiring. Every later temptation to promise cooling to a
shopkeeper would die against that list, and it did, twice.

Then came the theme work, and with it the run's first surprise. The broad theme,
"off-grid home power," described a market. The sharp theme, "paid-off-grid-light,"
described a need — light you pay for in small daily amounts, distinct from every
free-but-fleeting alternative. The team tagged the whole run sharp and never mixed the
forks. Months later, when someone proposed a grid-tie upsell, the tag is what killed
it in one sentence: that's the broad theme, not ours.

Enumeration was humbling. Six situations, and the discipline of three lenses per
situation, forced the team to admit what they didn't know. The cooling question at the
stall — S-0004 — sat there with an empty knowledge lens and an honest reason: cooling
is thermodynamic, not informational, and their 200W hub could not touch it. Writing
that down hurt. It also prevented the single most expensive mistake available to them,
which was discovering it in a headline.

Stakes made the arguments numerical. The stall's extra trading hour was worth sixty
dollars a month; a night fire could cost nine hundred. The evening-classes vote moved
no household money at all — a zero that the $0 rule forced them to flag and defend
rather than hide. When the designer later asked why the traders' materials carried
heavy certification lockups while the household pieces stayed plain, the answer was a
row in a CSV, not a taste: failure_cost 900 versus 180.

The reference fight ended the way joint runs end fights: with a test. Three candidate
images were scored against the top-ranked situations, and the matte-charcoal lantern
with one amber glow won not because it was prettiest but because it stretched —
bedroom, stall canopy, rooftop — while the others collapsed outside the bedroom. The
losers were filed, not mourned.

The incumbent catalog was the week's sober reading. Six ways customers already solved
these nights, including the rival blue packs that owned the dealer shelf and the dead
kerosene-subscription pilots in the graveyard. The fit pass then said the unsayable
out loud: full swap of the bedroom lantern, full swap of the kiosk queue,
query-face over mobile-money receipts — and an X against cooling, in writing, with a
reason. The overclaim guardrail earned its keep in the classroom row, where the swap
verdict shipped with certified fitting under the wiring code.

Buyer groups fell out of the fit table almost mechanically: mothers, traders, agents —
three groups, three briefs, each with hooks and a call to action in its own register.
The objection the team feared most, "looks like LuminaHome," was written into GRP-01
with its answer attached: the amber rail, the 7-day trial, the blue packs have no rail.
Fear, once written down with an answer, becomes a plan.

Only then — S5B done, handoff tables frozen — was anyone allowed to talk about beauty.
The brand brief imported the groups and the logic verbatim; nobody rewrote a word of
positioning to flatter a layout. Deconstruction split the lantern reference into what
could travel (one glow zone, matte-amber tension, dark negative space) and what could
not (everything blue, everything lantern-shaped, every borrowed claim). The direction
statement fit in a sentence: one amber rail of paid light in a matte charcoal world.

The first kit came back strong and slightly wrong — a second teal accent, a competing
icon set — and the audit did its job: keep the rail, remove the clutter, refine the
thickness rule. REFINE, not restart; the core was sound. Before the Anchor was locked,
the bridge check forced the money question one final time: did the traders' trust
weight show? did the livelihood rows stay honest? did the wording tiers render through
type, not through two kits? It did, and the Anchor locked.

Guidelines, key visual, three thread rows — bedroom, stall, rooftop — each group's
scene waiting for its brief's words. Brand Lock assigned every reference its seat.
The bedroom test asset came back CORRECTION REQUIRED: the label zone had drifted
blue-adjacent, toward the very rival the whole run was built to escape. One correction,
one re-audit, APPROVED. The team still talks about that catch, because it proved the
system could see what tired eyes at midnight could not.

Packaging views, final review, and then the measurement loop's first demand: a
prediction per scaled thread, falsifiable, with kill conditions. The bedroom flight
returned 1.6 trial requests per hundred against a 1.0 kill line, converting at plan.
SCALE. Confusion with LuminaHome sat at 4% — a watchlist entry, not a rework. The
findings flowed back to their owners: the trial rate into the stakes anchor, the
4% into prohibited styles, the Figma type-finishing fallback marked validated.

Adaeze keeps the GATES.log printed above her desk. Thirty-six green lines. Not because
green is the point — the fail fixture in `hooks/fixtures/` shows what red looks like —
but because every line is a decision nobody has to re-litigate. Taste still matters at
SunJar. It just no longer gets to vote before the evidence is in.
